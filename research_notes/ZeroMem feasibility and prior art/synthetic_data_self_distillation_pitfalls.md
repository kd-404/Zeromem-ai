# Synthetic Data & Self-Distillation Pitfalls for Small-Model Grounded-QA Fine-Tuning

## Self-distillation bias / model collapse risk when training data comes from another (or same-family) LLM

### Takeaway
Model collapse research shows that training generative models on model-generated data causes progressive loss of distributional diversity (tails/rare cases vanish, outputs regress to the mean), and is best documented for *recursive/iterative* self-training loops; the ~30M-parameter-student-from-3B-teacher case in this project is a **single-generation distillation**, not the recursive multi-generation loop the canonical papers study, so the mechanism transfers only partially and should be treated as an inferred risk, not a directly-measured one.

### Cited Findings
- Shumailov et al., "The Curse of Recursion: Training on Generated Data Makes Models Forget" (2023), show that when a generative model is trained on a corpus where a meaningful fraction of the data was produced by an earlier generation of the same kind of model, output distributions progressively narrow, losing rare/distinctive examples and regressing toward the statistical mean — termed "model collapse." — [arXiv:2305.17493](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17493)
- The follow-up Nature paper, Shumailov et al., "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data" (2024), finds indiscriminate use of model-generated content in training causes irreversible defects: tails of the original content distribution disappear, and using a Gaussian-estimation analysis shows the estimated covariance can collapse to zero over successive training iterations while error compounds via feedback loops. — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39048682/)
- A critical rebuttal, "A Note on Shumailov et al. (2024)," questions how universal/severe the collapse effect is and under what conditions it manifests, indicating the phenomenon is not unconditionally guaranteed and is sensitive to experimental setup (e.g., whether real data is retained alongside synthetic data across generations). — [arXiv:2410.12954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12954)
- In self-distillation (a model trained on its own past outputs, as opposed to human data), bias can be amplified because the model is pushed toward increasingly confident outputs; when a checkpoint of the same model is used as its own teacher, this confidence effect compounds over iterations. — [Lacuna summary of self-reinforcement literature](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/direction/mitigating-bias-in-self-improving-language-models/txn_8e3935464e4343dea0a175e0bcd8746e); related: "On the Amplification of Linguistic Bias through Unintentional Self-reinforcement Learning by Generative Language Models" — [arXiv:2306.07135](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.07135)
- "Bias Amplification in Language Model Evolution" (NeurIPS 2024) frames this as a self-reinforcing process: a model trained on synthetic data amplifies pre-existing biases from prior training, which then increases bias in the data it generates for the next iteration — again an *iterative* framing. — [NeurIPS proceedings PDF](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/4418f6a54f4314202688d77956e731ce-Paper-Conference.pdf)
- A 2026 paper, "Why Does Self-Distillation (Sometimes) Degrade the Reasoning Capability of LLMs?", and a related note on "On-Policy Self-Distillation with Sampled Demonstrations," both report that self-distillation setups can reduce output diversity when the teacher's guidance is conditioned on the model's own prior outputs/demonstrations, routing the learning signal through existing biases rather than treating all correct solution paths equally. — [arXiv:2603.24472](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.24472v1); [Pith summary of 2606.26091](https://pith.science/paper/2606.26091)
- Proposed mitigation in the self-improvement literature: explicit confidence calibration — forcing the model to evaluate its own certainty after rounds of self-improvement — helps preserve the ability to signal likely-wrong answers, i.e., directly relevant to a refusal/uncertainty-expression training goal. — [Lacuna](https://lacuna.tiptreesystems.com/direction/mitigating-bias-in-self-improving-language-models/txn_8e3935464e4343dea0a175e0bcd8746e)

### Inferences
- The canonical "model collapse" results (Shumailov et al.) are about *recursive* training — each generation's model is trained on the previous generation's outputs, repeated over many generations, and/or real data is progressively replaced by synthetic data. A single teacher (Qwen2.5-3B) generating a one-time synthetic dataset to train an unrelated, much smaller architecture (~30M params) is a different, milder setup: there is no iterative feedback loop and no risk of the *teacher itself* degrading over generations. The literature's collapse mechanism (loss of tail/rare examples, variance shrinkage) is still a plausible risk vector here — synthetic QA pairs from one teacher model will have a narrower stylistic and topical distribution than human-authored data — but the severity demonstrated in these papers should not be assumed to transfer 1:1 to a one-shot distillation setting.
- The more directly applicable risk for this project's setup is "teacher-style overfitting" / narrow distributional coverage (see also the "small model failure modes" section below) rather than literal multi-generation model collapse, since there is only one generation of synthetic-data training involved.
- The self-distillation-specific bias-amplification findings (confident-output amplification, reduced diversity when teacher is conditioned on its own demonstrations) are more directly relevant to *same-model* self-distillation loops than to a distinct larger-teacher/smaller-student pipeline, but they still support a general principle: uncalibrated teacher generation (e.g., a teacher that is always confident and rarely hedges) will teach the student the same overconfidence, which is directly counter to the refusal/uncertainty goal.

### Gaps
- No source directly studies model collapse or self-distillation bias in the specific configuration of this project: a ~3B-parameter teacher generating one-time synthetic data to fine-tune an unrelated ~30M-parameter student for a grounded-QA/citation task. All cited findings are extrapolated from either (a) same-architecture recursive generational studies, or (b) same-model self-distillation loops — not cross-scale, one-shot teacher-to-student distillation for a narrow refusal task. This is a significant gap and should be flagged as speculative extrapolation in any final report.
- No source quantifies how much a *single* round of teacher-generated data (as opposed to multi-generation recursive retraining) degrades diversity or calibration; the magnitude of risk for a one-shot pipeline is unclear from the literature and would need direct empirical testing.

## Best practices for generating high-quality synthetic QA/RAG training data with an LLM (diversity, avoiding repetition, verifying correctness)

### Takeaway
Established synthetic-data pipelines (Self-Instruct/Alpaca-style, Self-RAG, RAGTruth) consistently rely on three levers: (1) diverse seed/task sampling plus deduplication and quality filtering (often via a second LLM-as-judge pass) to avoid repetitive, low-diversity outputs; (2) using a strong model to produce structured intermediate signals (e.g., reflection/critique tokens) rather than raw free-text answers alone, to make correctness checkable; and (3) drawing base content from real, varied corpora (not just model imagination) to ground the diversity of contexts.

### Cited Findings
- The Self-Instruct/Alpaca pipeline generates instructions from a small human-written seed set by prompting a teacher LLM to produce diverse task descriptions and completions, then filters: removing duplicates of seed instructions, checking length bounds, requiring minimum token diversity, and validating pairs before acceptance. — [The Neural Base: Self-Instruct pipeline](https://theneuralbase.com/fine-tuning-fundamentals/learn/intermediate/self-instruct-pipeline/); [Eugene Yan, "How to Generate and Use Synthetic Data for Finetuning"](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/synthetic/)
- AlpaGasus applies a ChatGPT-based response-quality evaluator to filter Self-Instruct-generated instruction-response pairs, demonstrating LLM-as-judge filtering as a standard quality-control step on top of raw generation. — [The Neural Base](https://theneuralbase.com/fine-tuning-fundamentals/learn/intermediate/self-instruct-pipeline/)
- Deduplication is described as essential and "often skipped": if a large fraction of generated examples are near-duplicates, the result is low-diversity data and overfitting to a narrow distribution; practical implementations perform embedding-based similarity scans to catch and remove duplicates. — [Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/synthetic/)
- Synthetic data is recommended to be used "as a starting point that you then filter, deduplicate, and selectively augment with real labels on the hardest or most important examples," rather than as a complete, unfiltered substitute for human-labeled data. — [Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/synthetic/)
- Self-RAG's training data construction uses a strong proprietary LM (GPT-4) to generate reflection tokens (retrieval-need and critique signals) which are then used to supervise a separate Critic model; the Critic is subsequently used to insert reflection tokens into a broader text corpus, which becomes the Generator's training data. This two-stage design (strong teacher → distilled critic → labeled corpus) is intended to make the correctness/quality signal explicit and checkable rather than relying on raw LLM free-text generation alone. — [Self-RAG project page](https://selfrag.github.io/); [arXiv:2310.11511](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11511)
- RAGTruth's construction draws base content from established, diverse real-world corpora (MS MARCO for QA, Yelp Open Dataset for data-to-text, CNN/Daily Mail for summarization) and generates responses from six different LLMs (Llama2-7B/13B/70B, Mistral-7B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4), then applies meticulous human span-level annotation for hallucination — illustrating a best practice of using multiple generator models plus human verification rather than a single model's self-reported correctness. — [arXiv:2401.00396](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00396); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.585/)

### Inferences
- For a single 3B teacher generating both questions and grounded answers (with no separate human verification pass, as is implied by the project's ~30M-parameter budget), the RAGTruth/Self-RAG pattern of drawing base *content* from diverse real corpora (rather than having the LLM invent both the source text and the QA pair from scratch) is a directly applicable mitigation: it constrains the LLM's role to generation-conditioned-on-real-context, which limits how much stylistic/topical narrowing the synthetic data can introduce, versus a fully LLM-invented pipeline.
- The recurring emphasis on deduplication and LLM-as-judge filtering across Self-Instruct/Alpagasus and Self-RAG's Critic-model design suggests a two-pass generation pipeline (generate, then filter/verify with either the same or a second model/heuristic) is the practical minimum bar for a single-teacher synthetic dataset, given no source describes a single-pass ungoverned LLM generation pipeline as sufficient for quality.

### Gaps
- No source specifically evaluates or recommends synthetic-data generation practices at the scale of the eventual dataset needed for a ~30M-parameter student (likely far smaller total data volume than Self-Instruct/Alpaca's 52K examples or RAGTruth's ~18K annotated examples); best-practice guidance found is general to LLM fine-tuning rather than tailored to very small final datasets.
- No source directly addresses using a *3B*-scale (rather than GPT-3.5/GPT-4-scale) teacher for QA-pair generation and whether a smaller teacher's lower generation quality changes the calculus on how much filtering/verification is needed; this should be treated as a gap requiring caution — a 3B model teacher is meaningfully weaker than the GPT-4-class teachers used in Self-RAG and AlpaGasus, so those pipelines' filtering intensity may understate what is needed here.

## Best practice for constructing "unanswerable"/negative examples for genuine refusal/abstention learning

### Takeaway
The established pattern (from SQuAD 2.0 and refusal-tuning papers like R-Tuning) is to make unanswerable examples *look like* answerable ones — same topic, same passage, plausible-seeming question — rather than obviously irrelevant, so the model learns to detect genuine absence of the answer in context rather than superficial topic mismatch; R-Tuning further grounds refusal in the model's own demonstrated knowledge boundary rather than arbitrary labels.

### Cited Findings
- SQuAD 2.0 combines the original SQuAD 1.1 answerable questions with over 50,000 unanswerable questions written adversarially by crowdworkers "to look similar to answerable ones" — crowdworkers were shown an SQuAD 1.1 article and asked to write questions with a plausible answer in mind that are relevant to the passage but cannot actually be answered from it, making unanswerable questions deliberately hard to distinguish from answerable ones. — [arXiv:1806.03822](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.03822); [emergentmind summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/1806.03822)
- SQuAD 2.0's train split has roughly twice as many answerable as unanswerable questions, while dev/test splits use roughly a 1:1 ratio of answerable to unanswerable. — [arXiv:1806.03822](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.03822)
- R-Tuning ("Refusal-Aware Instruction Tuning") argues that prior instruction-tuning approaches force models to complete an answer regardless of whether the model actually has the requisite knowledge, causing hallucination when the question is outside the model's parametric knowledge; R-Tuning instead first identifies the disparity between what's in the pretrained model's parameters and what the instruction-tuning data assumes, then constructs refusal-aware training data from that knowledge intersection so refusal is tied to genuine knowledge gaps rather than arbitrary or blanket refusal. — [arXiv:2311.09677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09677); [ACL Anthology (NAACL 2024 Outstanding Paper)](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.394/)
- R-Tuning's experiments show it improves both the ability to answer known questions and the ability to refrain from answering unknown ones, and that on out-of-domain datasets the refusal behavior generalizes as a "meta-skill" transferable beyond the training distribution. — [arXiv:2311.09677](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09677)
- In a directly RAG-relevant construction (FinRAG-12B, a production-oriented grounded-QA recipe), unanswerable examples were built by pairing a user query with retrieved context that is "topically relevant but lacks sufficient information to support a correct answer" — i.e., near-miss/distractor-style unanswerable contexts, not off-topic ones — with the target output being an explicit "I don't know" response; each training example included 1–2 relevant and 3–4 distractor passages alongside human-annotated reference answers and a binary answerability label. — [arXiv:2605.05482](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.05482)
- FinRAG-12B reports that training with 22% unanswerable examples (roughly a 78:22 answerable:unanswerable ratio) produced a calibrated 12% "I don't know" rate on their held-out test distribution, versus an unsafe 4.3% refusal rate for their un-tuned base model (under-refusing) and a 20.2% over-refusal rate for GPT-4.1 as a comparison point — framed as evidence that both too little and too much refusal training data produce miscalibrated behavior. — [arXiv:2605.05482](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.05482)

### Inferences
- Both SQuAD 2.0 and the RAG-specific FinRAG-12B construction converge on the same principle: negative/unanswerable examples should be "near-miss" (topically on-target, contextually plausible, but genuinely missing the needed fact), not trivially off-topic — because training on trivially-off-topic negatives teaches the model to detect topic mismatch rather than genuine information absence, which would fail to generalize to the harder real-world case of a relevant-but-incomplete retrieved chunk.
- R-Tuning's approach of grounding refusal in the model's actual demonstrated knowledge boundary (rather than a synthetic label applied post hoc) is harder to directly replicate in a chunk-grounded QA setting, since here "knowledge" is defined by the provided context chunk rather than the model's parametric knowledge — but the underlying principle (don't force answer synthesis when the necessary evidence isn't present) is the same and is the more directly transferable lesson for this project.

### Gaps
- The FinRAG-12B paper (arXiv:2605.05482) was only checked via search-engine and WebFetch summarization, not a full independent read of the PDF; verify the 22% figure and its generalizability directly if it will be relied on for a specific ratio decision, since this appears to be a narrow, single-paper data point rather than a broadly replicated finding.
- No source specifically addresses how to construct unanswerable examples via an *LLM generator itself* (i.e., asking a 3B teacher to generate a plausible-but-unanswerable question for a given chunk) as opposed to human-crafted ones (SQuAD 2.0) or retrieval-based near-miss pairing (FinRAG-12B); whether an LLM teacher can reliably generate genuinely difficult/plausible negatives (versus negatives that are secretly answerable or trivially unanswerable) is an open methodological question not covered by any source found.

## Known failure modes when fine-tuning a very small (~30M parameter) model vs. billion-plus models on instruction/QA data

### Takeaway
Available evidence suggests capacity constraints bite well before billion-parameter scale — instruction tuning has been reported to hurt generalization to unseen tasks specifically for models around 8B parameters and below — and separately, very small models fine-tuned on limited synthetic data have been observed to struggle with generating exact required phrasing, indicating a hard capacity ceiling; however, no source directly addresses ~30M-parameter models, which are roughly 250x smaller than the smallest scale (8B) discussed in the clearest source found, so this section is the most speculative/extrapolated of the five.

### Cited Findings
- Research on instruction-tuning scaling (referenced as studying LaMDA-PT) found that instruction tuning actually *hurts* generalization to unseen tasks for models at 8B parameters and smaller, and that instruction-tuned models only begin to significantly outperform their non-instruction-tuned counterparts once model size reaches 68B parameters — attributed to all available model capacity being consumed by learning the mixture of instruction-tuning tasks itself rather than generalizing from it. — [search-result synthesis; primary source likely Wei et al., "Finetuned Language Models Are Zero-Shot Learners"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.01652) — **note: this specific claim could not be independently re-verified by direct PDF text extraction (the fetch of the PDF failed to yield readable text); treat as a search-engine-summarized claim pending verification against the original paper's figures.**
- Small Language Models (SLMs) are broadly categorized in industry/survey material as ranging from about 100 million to 5 billion parameters; a ~30M-parameter model falls below even this commonly-cited SLM range, i.e., is smaller than what most current "small model" fine-tuning literature actually studies. — [Omdena, "A Practical Guide to Fine-Tuning Small Language Models"](https://www.omdena.com/blog/fine-tuning-small-language-models); [PremAI blog on fine-tuning SLMs](https://www.premai.io/blog/fine-tuning-small-language-models/)
- For very small models fine-tuned on small datasets (500–2,000 records cited in one source), increasing LoRA parameters did not overcome limited effectiveness — the model "struggled to generate exact phrases," which was interpreted as a sign of limited learning capacity relative to dataset complexity. — [search-result synthesis of small-model fine-tuning literature; specific paper not individually identified in the results returned]

### Inferences
- If instruction tuning already shows negative generalization effects at 8B parameters (a scale ~270x larger than 30M), it is reasonable to infer — but not confirmed by any direct source — that a 30M-parameter model has even less spare capacity to (a) parse multi-part instructions (e.g., "answer using only the provided chunk, cite the source, and refuse if the answer isn't present" as a compound instruction), and (b) generalize a refusal *skill* across varied unseen contexts rather than merely memorizing surface patterns from the synthetic training set. This makes the "near-miss unanswerable examples" and diversity/deduplication best practices (from the sections above) more, not less, important for a 30M model, since a small model has less capacity to interpolate past narrow or repetitive training signal.
- The observation that small models "struggle to generate exact phrases" when fine-tuned on small synthetic datasets suggests that for a citation/grounded-QA task specifically (which likely requires precise span-copying or citation-formatting), dataset size and exactness of formatting in the synthetic examples may matter more for a 30M model than instruction diversity alone.

### Gaps
- No source studies fine-tuning behavior specifically at the ~10M–50M parameter range for instruction-style or QA/refusal tasks; all cited findings are extrapolated downward from 8B-and-above (instruction-tuning scaling) or from unspecified "very small model" fine-tuning blog-level sources whose exact parameter counts, architectures, and paper identities were not resolved in the search results returned. This entire section should be flagged in any downstream report as **inferred/extrapolated, not directly evidenced**, given the ~250x-plus gap between the smallest studied scale found (8B) and the project's actual target (~30M).
- The claim about LaMDA-PT / 68B threshold could not be verified against the original paper text (PDF extraction failed); it should be re-verified against Wei et al. 2021 ("Finetuned Language Models Are Zero-Shot Learners," arXiv:2109.01652) figures directly before being cited as a hard fact in any final report.

## Recommended ratio of answerable-to-unanswerable examples and data volume for comparable refusal-training / grounded-QA setups

### Takeaway
The clearest concrete data point found (FinRAG-12B) used roughly a 78:22 answerable:unanswerable ratio and a total training budget of 143M tokens to reach calibrated refusal behavior; SQuAD 2.0, the most established general precedent, used roughly a 2:1 answerable:unanswerable ratio in training data (moving to roughly 1:1 in dev/test) — both indicate unanswerable examples should be a meaningful minority (roughly one-fifth to one-third) rather than a rare edge case or a near-even split, but the evidence base for a precise "right" ratio is thin and setup-specific.

### Cited Findings
- SQuAD 2.0 training data has roughly twice as many answerable as unanswerable questions (~2:1 ratio), while dev and test sets use close to a 1:1 ratio of answerable to unanswerable. — [arXiv:1806.03822](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.03822)
- FinRAG-12B's calibrated grounded-QA model was trained with 22% of examples unanswerable (~78:22 ratio) and a total of 143M training tokens, and this configuration produced a 12% refusal rate on their evaluation set — described as substantially better-calibrated than both an under-refusing base model (4.3% refusal rate) and an over-refusing GPT-4.1 comparison (20.2% refusal rate). — [arXiv:2605.05482](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.05482)
- Self-Instruct/Alpaca-style pipelines commonly target on the order of tens of thousands of generated examples (Alpaca's well-known release used 52K instruction-following examples) as a reference point for what "sufficient volume" has looked like for instruction-tuning smaller (7B-scale) models from a strong teacher, though this is a general instruction-tuning volume rather than a refusal-specific one. — [The Neural Base](https://theneuralbase.com/fine-tuning-fundamentals/learn/intermediate/alpaca-used-this/)
- RAGTruth, a hallucination/grounded-generation benchmark (not itself a fine-tuning training set, but a comparably-scoped RAG-grounding resource), contains about 18,000 annotated examples across QA, data-to-text, and summarization tasks combined — offered here only as a scale reference for "comparably-scoped RAG resource sizes," not as a direct answerable/unanswerable ratio recommendation. — [arXiv:2401.00396](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00396)

### Inferences
- Triangulating SQuAD 2.0 (~2:1, i.e., ~33% unanswerable) and FinRAG-12B (~78:22, i.e., 22% unanswerable), a reasonable target range for a grounded-QA refusal training set appears to be roughly **20–35% unanswerable examples**, with exact placement likely depending on how "hard"/near-miss the negatives are (harder negatives may need a smaller share to achieve the same calibration effect, per FinRAG-12B's use of deliberately topically-relevant distractor-style negatives).
- None of the volume figures found (52K for Alpaca, ~18K for RAGTruth, 143M tokens for FinRAG-12B) are from a setup matching this project's small (~30M parameter) student model; given the capacity-limit findings in the previous section, it is plausible that a 30M-parameter model needs *less* total data to saturate its learning capacity than these larger-model examples imply, but no source directly supports a specific smaller number — this is an inference, not a cited fact.

### Gaps
- No source gives a data-volume or ratio recommendation specifically calibrated to a small-parameter-count (tens-of-millions) student model; all ratio/volume figures found come from setups fine-tuning or benchmarking models at 7B+ (Alpaca/Self-Instruct) or 12B (FinRAG-12B) scale, or from a human-crowdsourced dataset (SQuAD 2.0) not tied to any particular model size. Any ratio/volume number adopted from this research should be treated as a starting-point heuristic to be validated empirically on the actual 30M-parameter student, not a settled best practice.
- The FinRAG-12B paper was accessed only via search snippet and one WebFetch summarization pass, not a full manual read; if this ratio is to anchor a design decision, it warrants direct verification of the paper's methodology section (e.g., whether the 22% figure was itself tuned via ablation, or was an arbitrary choice) before treating it as more than a single supporting data point.
